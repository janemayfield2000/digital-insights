Building a Correction Workflow: The Part of Directory Management Nobody Plans For

Ask most teams to plan a directory campaign and they will describe the submissions in great detail: which directories, how many, in what order. Ask them what happens when a listing turns out to be wrong, and the answer is usually a pause followed by something vague. Yet corrections are where a large share of the real work lives, and the quality of the correction workflow often determines whether a campaign succeeds.

The reason corrections get neglected is simple. Submissions feel like progress and corrections feel like admitting a mistake. In reality, problems are an ordinary part of managing listings on third-party platforms, and a team that expects them and has a plan will outperform a team that treats each one as a surprise.

Corrections Are Slower Than Submissions

Submitting a listing is a repeatable task that can be done in batches. Correcting one is closer to investigation. Someone has to notice the problem, figure out where the wrong information came from, fix it at the source, apply the update to every record that inherited it and then confirm that the fix actually took effect.

This asymmetry has a consequence that is easy to miss: if submissions scale faster than corrections can be handled, the backlog grows no matter how hard the team works. The correct response is not to work harder but to slow submissions down until correction capacity catches up, which is exactly why a healthy workflow measures capacity before it expands.

The Elements of a Working Workflow

A good correction workflow has a few simple parts. It has a named owner for fixes and a backup who can take over, so the queue never depends on a single person. It has a single place where problems are recorded, so nothing lives only in someone's inbox. And it has a clear sense of severity, so the most serious issues are handled first and cannot quietly age at the back of the line.

It also has a definition of closed. A problem is not solved when someone says it is fixed; it is solved when the change has been verified and the issue does not return. Tracking whether closed issues reopen is one of the most revealing signals available, because repeated reopens point to a root cause that the workflow has not yet reached.

Watching the Queue, Not Just the Output

Once the workflow exists, it needs to be watched. The most useful signs are the age of the oldest unresolved issues, the speed at which new issues are closed, and the reopen trend. When aging rises week over week, or closure pace drops below target, those are warnings that the process is under strain, even if the number of live listings is still growing impressively.

A fixed review rhythm makes these signs hard to ignore. Review the queue first, review quality second, and only then consider whether the scope can safely widen. If the first two reviews look unstable, the answer to the third is simply no.

The Role of Visibility

All of this depends on being able to see what is happening. A workflow that hides status, whether inside one person's spreadsheet or a provider's closed system, makes every other control weaker. Clear reporting of what has been submitted and what is pending, along with who owns each open fix, is what turns a correction process from a hope into a habit.

The Bottom Line

Plan your corrections as seriously as your submissions. Name owners, record every issue in one place, define what closed really means and watch the queue as closely as the output. A campaign built on a strong correction workflow can grow steadily, while one without it spends most of its time putting out fires.
